<!-- block: open -->

On a Tuesday in October, some years ago, I got up, made coffee, drove to work, came home, cooked something with rice in it, watched forty minutes of a show I have completely forgotten, and went to bed.

I remember that day perfectly.

I could not tell you what happened at my fortieth birthday, or at two of the three weddings I know I attended that decade. But I can tell you what the kitchen light was doing at six o'clock, and that nobody said anything remarkable, and that I have thought about that evening maybe two hundred times since.

<!-- block: flow-1a -->

This bothered me for years, because it does not fit the shape we are taught.

We are taught that a life is made of events. Graduations, promotions, weddings, births, the trip you saved two years for. These are the load-bearing beams. Everything between them is drywall. The events go in the album; the rest is what you get through on the way to the next one.

It is a tidy model. It is also almost entirely wrong about how a life is actually experienced.

Start with the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is brutal and clarifying. If you are lucky, you get somewhere around thirty thousand days. Of those, the number that will end up in the album — the genuine peaks, the ones with photographs and speeches and a version of you in good shoes — might be two hundred. Call it three hundred if you have had an unusually eventful life.

That leaves twenty-nine thousand seven hundred.

If the ordinary days are not the point, then a life is ninety-nine percent waiting room. You would be spending nearly the whole thing in the corridor outside the important part. I do not believe anyone actually lives that way. I do believe a great many of us describe our lives that way, which is its own kind of problem.

Here is what I think is happening instead. The peaks are not the life. The peaks are the punctuation. What you are actually made of is Tuesday.

<!-- block: flow-1b -->

Memory seems to know this even when we don't.

Ask someone to describe a person they loved and lost, and watch what comes out. It is almost never the wedding. It is the way they stood at the sink. A specific laugh. What they used to say about the neighbors. The sound of them coming in the door, which you did not know you were memorizing at the time and now cannot stop hearing.

Grief is unbearably specific, and what it is specific about is ordinary. That should tell us something. Whatever part of us decides what to keep is not impressed by events. It keeps texture.

I suspect this is because ordinary days are the only place you live at full resolution. A wedding is compressed — you are performing, managing, aware of being watched, running a small logistics operation in your head. A Tuesday has nothing to perform. It is the only stretch of time you are ever unobserved enough to be entirely yourself, which is presumably why it is the version that survives.

<!-- block: field-note -->

Thirty thousand days, at roughly eighty-two years. In rust, two hundred of them: the birthdays, the ceremonies, the trips, everything with a photograph. Look at how little of the field it takes.

<!-- block: flow-2a -->

None of this is an argument for lowering your standards.

I am not saying you should stop wanting things, or that ambition is a mistake, or that the trip is not worth taking. Take the trip. I am saying something narrower and, I think, more useful. The trip will occupy about nine days of your life, and the anticipation and the retelling maybe another thirty, and then you will be back in the kitchen. The kitchen is not the price of the trip. The kitchen is the life the trip is an interruption in.

<!-- block: flow-2b -->

There is an inversion available here that I keep losing and keep coming back to. Most of us treat ordinary days as the raw material and extraordinary days as the product. Try running it the other way. The extraordinary days are the raw material — the fuel, the story, the reason — and the ordinary day is the thing being built. A good ordinary day is not a day on which nothing happened. It is a day on which the thing you have been building actually worked.

You can tell when it works, too. It does not feel like happiness exactly. It feels like the absence of wanting to be somewhere else.

<!-- block: flow-3a -->

The trouble with ordinary days is that they are almost impossible to notice while they are happening and trivially easy to notice afterward.

This is not a character flaw. It is structural. An ordinary day has no edges. Nothing announces it. There is no photograph, no toast, no one standing up to say a few words. It arrives unmarked and it leaves unmarked, and the only reason I still have that Tuesday in October is that something in me happened to be awake for ninety seconds while the light was doing what it was doing.

I have come to think this is the whole practical problem of being alive, and that it is a great deal smaller than the philosophical version. You do not need to determine the meaning of life. You need a slightly better mechanism for noticing the parts of it that are already good, while they are still going on.

<!-- block: flow-3b -->

I do not have a reliable mechanism. I have a few unreliable ones.

Cooking helps, because it is slow and has a smell. Walking helps. So does a dog, who draws no distinction between a Tuesday and a birthday and is, on this particular question, better informed than I am. Doing something badly on purpose helps — badly enough that you have to attend to it. And saying out loud, in an ordinary room, that this is nice, which feels foolish and works anyway.

None of that is a practice. It is a handful of things that occasionally interrupt the automatic processing long enough for a day to register as a day.

<!-- block: close -->

I still do not know what happened at those weddings.

I know that the kitchen light in October is orange in a way it is not in any other month, and that a room can be completely quiet and completely full at the same time, and that I was there for it.

There will be a last ordinary Tuesday. It will look exactly like the others.
